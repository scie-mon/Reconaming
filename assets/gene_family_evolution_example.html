An illustrative evolutionary history

How does gene family evolution work?

A simplified, fictitious history of a multigene family, beginning with one gene in a single ancestral species.

This example follows gene lineages through speciation, duplication, and loss. Each tip represents a gene locus; allelic variation and other evolutionary processes are not considered. We initially know the complete history, allowing us to compare what actually happened with what could be inferred from the surviving genes.

The species tree and gene tree are shown side by side. The diagrams show topology only. Unlabelled nodes with only one child are omitted from the drawings, but retained in the original Newick strings below each tree.

Gene-tree branches use purple and orange by default. Activate “Show Species Colors” to colour the branches by species instead; tip labels retain their species colours in either mode. Filled orange diamonds mark visible duplications. A hollow, dashed diamond marks the hidden duplication known from the constructed history.

Reciprocal gene losses

Now suppose SpeciesA loses Gene5, while SpeciesB loses Gene6 and Gene7. We know from the constructed history that the surviving SpB-Gene5 and SpA-Gene6 are paralogs.

These reciprocal losses hide the earlier duplication. The surviving topology alone cannot distinguish this history from a speciation, so an analysis relying on that topology would treat the surviving genes as orthologs. Recovering the duplication requires independent evidence that distinguishes the two histories.

The loss of Gene7 in SpeciesB is separate from the reciprocal losses that hide the duplication.

Gene8 duplicates in SpeciesC

Next, suppose Gene8 duplicates in SpeciesC, producing Gene9 and Gene10:

The remaining Gene8 copies in SpeciesA, SpeciesE, and SpeciesF are essentially the same gene in different species. Gene9 and Gene10, however, are separately distinguished genes created by the duplication in SpeciesC.

Yet both Gene9 and Gene10 are orthologous to each of those external Gene8 copies per definition: the most recent common ancestor of each such pair is a speciation. Gene9 and Gene10 are paralogs of one another.

This is the crucial distinction: orthology includes both the “same gene in different species” correspondence and one-to-many relationships involving later duplicates. Orthology alone therefore does not express the narrower gene identity we want the names to convey.

The tips still called Gene8 also no longer form a complete clade: their common ancestor gave rise to Gene9 and Gene10 as well.

SpeciesC splits into SpeciesG and SpeciesH

Next, suppose SpeciesC splits into SpeciesG and SpeciesH:

The Gene9 copies in SpeciesG and SpeciesH are now the same gene in different species, as are the two Gene10 copies. Both pairs remain part of the broader ancestral Gene8 lineage.

For example, SpA-Gene8 and SpE-Gene8 represent the same gene in different species. SpH-Gene9 and SpE-Gene8 do not represent that same gene identity, although they too are orthologs. A useful naming scheme should preserve this distinction.

A practical naming compromise

The boundary logic already lets us distinguish groups representing the same gene in different species: we keep a shared identity along speciation branches and distinguish new identities across duplication boundaries. We could stop here and assign an independent number to each of these naming groups.

But independent numbers would communicate nothing about the broader evolutionary relationships between the groups. Encoding every nested relationship would require a hierarchy of identifiers following successive duplications. Such names would quickly become cumbersome, especially as new data become available and inferred histories change.

We therefore adopt a practical compromise: retain a single broader grouping level, defined relative to a chosen ancestral reference point within a predefined, fixed taxonomic scope—for example, a particular genus. Each ancestral gene lineage at that reference point defines a ParentGroup which can be identified by locating the first non-duplication nodes within that selected taxonomic focus. This follows the principle of hierarchical orthologous groups (HOGs). The leading number identifies the ParentGroup, while a suffix distinguishes the naming groups within it.

The grouping therefore depends on the evolutionary history included in the input. If we widened the focus to include an older speciation preceding the duplications shown here, all genes in our present example could fall within a single ParentGroup and share one leading number. With the narrower focus used here, several gene lineages have already separated by duplication before the first speciation and therefore define separate ParentGroups. Changing the input scope changes this grouping, not the underlying orthology or paralogy relationships.

Applied to the Gene8 lineage, this gives the following names:

The copies in SpeciesA, SpeciesE, and SpeciesF receive Gene8-1. The Gene9 descendants in SpeciesG and SpeciesH become Gene8-2, and the Gene10 descendants become Gene8-3. Their shared leading number records membership in the same ParentGroup; their complete identifiers distinguish the narrower gene identities.

Thus, the same complete identifier—ignoring the species prefix—denotes the same inferred gene identity across species. A shared leading number denotes the broader ancestral grouping. Different suffixes do not automatically mean paralogy: SpAGene8-1 remains orthologous to both SpHGene8-2 and SpHGene8-3. The suffix can be omitted when a ParentGroup has only one naming group.

Extending naming boundaries to uncertain relationships

So far, we have used duplication nodes as boundaries between naming groups. In a reconstructed phylogeny, however, the branching relationships themselves may be uncertain. A node not identified as a duplication therefore does not automatically provide a sufficiently reliable connection for assigning a shared gene identity.

We extend the boundary logic accordingly: in addition to duplication nodes, insufficiently supported non-duplication nodes can act as naming boundaries. A chosen support threshold determines whether such a node allows a shared naming assignment to continue across it or separates the connected lineages into different naming groups. This makes uncertainty part of the naming procedure rather than something considered only after names have been assigned.

The two types of boundary have different meanings. A duplication boundary distinguishes gene identities on the basis of the inferred evolutionary history. A low-support boundary instead indicates that the relationship is too uncertain to justify a shared identity; it does not assert that a duplication occurred. This conservative choice reduces the risk of incorrectly merging distinct gene lineages, at the cost of sometimes assigning separate names to genes that would otherwise share one.
